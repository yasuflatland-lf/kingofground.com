This page is compiled from the KOG 10th anniversary book and covers 1998 through 2008. The list of series champions at the end runs through 2016, based on the year-end standings kept by the organizers. The year-by-year record from 2009 on will be added here as the materials are put together.

## Before KOG — how BMX took root in Japan

BMX arrived in Japan in the 1970s, and it was Kozo Hiraki who set its history in motion here. Coverage in the popular magazines of the day spread the sport and brought on the first BMX boom. Under Hiraki's lead the JBFA was formed, series contests were organised around Tokyo, and a second generation of riders gathered at them.

Those second-generation riders drove BMX further: the magazine _MOVIN' ON_, published alongside other action sports, the STREET MEET contests on Harajuku's pedestrian-only street, and growing television coverage. BMX at the time was split into only two categories, race and freestyle, and freestyle riders enjoyed flatland — then called ground tricks — and park riding without separating the two.

When the JBFA came to an end in the late 1980s, riders who had lost the centre of the scene began building organisations of their own. In Tokyo, Takeshi Ishihara founded the JFA and held contests at Komazawa Park at irregular intervals. In western Japan, Shigeji Fumihira ran contests under the same JFA name, and the two organisations later merged into the second period of the JFA. Promotion picked up in other cities as well, and the exchange between riders started to pull the regional scenes into something national.

The JFA held its last contest in 1996, but contests carried on across the country: the Dera-Uma Cup by Shotaro Teshigahara in Nagoya, the Komazawa jams by former JFA member Keiji Sasaki, and the B-1 Grand Prix and Akiru Jam run by Jun Otsuhata and the Ito brothers around Tokyo. Around the same time, the riders born in 1976–78 — Takashi Ito and York Uno among them — began entering the BS Contest series run by Hoffman Bikes in the United States, then the highest level in the world. That was the start of Japanese riders competing seriously in contest series abroad.

## 1998–2000 — It started at ROX3 in Asakusa

In the late 1990s the Japanese scene had no contest at its centre. Takashi Ito and Kotaro Tanaka had been talking about wanting a contest that would bring riders from all over the country together to decide a national champion; York Uno joined them, and the three founded KING OF GROUND. The name came back from Ito, built on a suggestion of "KINGS" from Atsushi "SharLee" Ishitani.

The first contest was held on 23 August 1998 at the ROX3 Super Multi Court in Asakusa, Tokyo, as a one-off exhibition ahead of the series planned for the following year. There were only two classes then, HI and LOW. The winner was Nathan Penonzek, the sole entry from overseas, whose two-minute run without a single break left a deep impression on a Japanese scene that was still finding its feet. From that day, ROX3 became the riders' sanctuary.

In 1999, KOG started properly as a four-round series. Entries grew with every round, and the series became something riders across Japan aimed at. At the final round, back at ROX3 in Tokyo, Akira Okamura took the top of the podium with the words "Bring it on!" and became the first King Of Ground. That same year he placed 7th at the world championships in Germany and appeared on the cover of the German BMX magazine _FREEDOM BMX_.

The 2000 season came down to Okamura and Takashi Ito. Ito won Round 1 and carried that form to the X-Games, where he and York Uno became the first Japanese flatland riders to compete and to reach the final. Okamura took Round 2 and led the standings after Round 3, but at the final round at ROX3 Ito finished 2nd, just ahead of Okamura, and took the title at the last possible moment. He closed with Okamura's own line, "Bring it on!", and became the second King Of Ground. In those years the organisers and top riders argued late into the night before and after every contest, and the scoring method and format still in use today took shape out of those arguments.

## 2001 — The era of the "Top 6"

By its third year KOG was firmly part of the scene. The riders known as the Top 6 — Akira Okamura, Takashi Ito, York Uno, Hiroya Morizaki, Ryoji Yamamoto and Kotaro Tanaka — were touring the United States and Europe, and high placings at contests abroad had stopped being unusual.

More than 150 riders came from all over Japan to the small space at LOFT in Nagoya. The champion that year was Hiroya Morizaki, who worked a large number of hard switches into long routines and held off the field with exceptional physical ability and concentration.

The final round at Twin21 in Osaka drew Viki Gomez, Alex Jumelin and many other riders from abroad, and the crew from France's _Cream_ magazine came to Japan for the first time to cover KOG. This was the year "the world" stopped being a distant idea and started being talked about as a real goal.

## 2002 — The rest of the world starts paying attention

By 2002, Japanese riders were regulars at contests around the world. It was not only their results: they made enough of an impression to leave riders abroad genuinely curious, and curious enough to come and see the "flatland capital" for themselves. This is also when KOG began to be recognised as one of the contests that mattered in the world scene.

Ryoji Yamamoto, known as Yanmer, won Rounds 2 and 3 back to back and became the fourth King Of Ground. He took the title riding the "waving move" he had invented, worked properly into his routines — a move riders around the world would soon adopt.

## 2003 — Kotaro Tanaka's long-awaited title

In 2003 the X-Games, the contest riders everywhere dreamed of reaching, decided to drop flatland after that year. The centre of flatland had already begun shifting to Europe and Japan. Against that backdrop KOG recorded its largest entry yet at around 200 riders, and took on real weight as the backbone of the scene.

The final round at ROX3 in Tokyo drew a strong international field, including that year's X-Games gold medallist Simon O'Brien, Matt Wilhelm and Travis Collier. Four of the Top 6 already held series titles; Kotaro Tanaka, the one who did not, finally reached the top and became the fifth series champion.

## 2004 — Ryoji Yamamoto sweeps the world

From 2004 the series was cut to three rounds a year. At the same time KOG began holding rounds outside the major metropolitan areas, with the aim of making contests at KOG's level possible anywhere in Japan. The first of these was in Niigata, run successfully thanks to the work of local pro rider Yutaka Tamaki, and it became the starting point for a generation of staff who would go on to support KOG.

Japanese riders had a remarkable year internationally. Akira Okamura, the first KOG champion, became the first Japanese winner of the world championships in Germany. Ryoji Yamamoto won Flatground in the Netherlands and FISE in France back to back, and was named best flatland rider at the NORA Cup hosted by the American magazine Ride BMX. Above all, he won Circle of Balance in Germany, the biggest and highest-level contest in the world. He also won Round 2 of KOG and took his second series title.

## 2005 — Dramas born in new places

Rounds in Fukushima and Kobe made 2005 a year of new places and new stars. Takuma Kawamura, who had worked his way up from Expert to Pro with a style all his own, went toe to toe with world-class rider Viki Gomez in Round 1. In Round 2, Yohei Uchino — who had done the work to bring the round to his home town of Kobe — won his first KOG landing trick after trick that he rarely pulled even in practice. Kawamura placed 2nd in Kobe and 7th in the final round, but his consistency in the finals earned him his first championship.

The organisation changed, too. Since the beginning KOG had been recruiting hands at the venue for every contest; as the regional rounds went on, more people travelled with the series as dedicated staff, and the quality of the operation rose sharply. In Fukushima, Tomoya Watanabe started by finding a local sponsor and, in his second year, secured the first regional title sponsor in KOG's history. An organisation that had not so much as met the people at its sponsor companies began talking to them directly, and the work shifted from volunteering to a job.

Riders from abroad kept coming, and KOG footage was included in _Joe Kid on a Stingray_, Mark Eaton's documentary on the history of BMX, released that year. KOG was moving from "the contest that decides the best in Japan" to a contest held to world standards.

## 2006 — The battle format and a new generation

With contests around the world moving to a one-on-one battle format, KOG adopted it as well, weighing both how riders would progress under it and how the contest would come across to an audience.

It was also the year of the so-called "KOG children" — riders in their teens and early twenties for whom KOG had always existed — alongside the mid-career riders. In Round 1, Yoshihiro "Taka" Nishikawa of Hiroshima beat the veteran Kotaro Tanaka. In Round 2, Moto Sasaki of the next generation edged out another veteran, York Uno. Viki Gomez took the final round, his most successful year among the international riders, but York Uno — the last of the Top 6 without a title — finished first in the overall standings and finally took the championship.

## 2007 — Circle of Balance and the World Circuit

2007 was a turning point for the Japanese scene. The size and depth of Japan's flatland population, the largest anywhere, made it possible to hold Red Bull Circle of Balance, the biggest and highest-level flatland contest in the world, in Japan. York Uno, Hiroya Morizaki, Kotaro Tanaka, Ryoji Yamamoto and Yoshihiro Nishikawa were selected among the world's best riders, and the KOG crew ran the event. It drew an audience of more than 1,100, and Morizaki finished 2nd after a close final against Viki Gomez.

The same year, the first round of the BMX Flatland World Circuit was held in Japan alongside KOG Round 1. The series brought together contests run by riders for riders, including Flatground in the Netherlands and Voodoo Jam in the United States. More than ten Japanese riders travelled to the final round at Flatground, where Hiroya Morizaki held on to become the first world circuit champion.

The Pro final at the KOG season closer in Asakusa came down to Yoshiki Uchino, an 18-year-old rookie pro, and Morizaki, the new world champion. Both rode everything they had, past the end of the allotted time, and Uchino's long, technically extreme routine took it. He finished his first year in Pro as champion, with the most round wins of the season and as the youngest champion at 18 — a fitting way to close the tenth year.

## 2008 — A contest at world standards

Hirokazu Miura of Nagoya won Round 2 in Komatsu, and the championship went to the final round with defending champion Yoshiki Uchino and the fast-rising Moto Sasaki tied at the top. The next generation had taken the stage at KOG.

The final round at ROX in Asakusa doubled as the final round of the second BMX Flatland World Circuit. Seventeen riders from eight countries took part, and the Pro final was the highest level of riding in KOG's ten years. The world champion was Matthias Dandois, 19 years old.

Yohei Uchino won Voodoo Jam in the United States, beating the rider then considered the strongest in the world, and became the first rider outside the Top 6 to win an international contest. He finished 3rd in the world rankings. The KOG series closed with Hiroya Morizaki taking his second championship, seven years after his first in 2001.
